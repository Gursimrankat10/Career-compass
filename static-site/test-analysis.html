ANSWER-BY-ANSWER ANALYSIS

What went well and what to revisit

✓ Correct

Two reports give different national employment figures. One counts people employed at any point in a year; the other counts people employed in a survey week. What is the first sound comparison step?

Your answer: Align the population, reference period, definitions, and collection methods before comparing the numbers.

Different definitions and reference periods measure different quantities; they must be reconciled before the values can be compared.

✓ Correct

A pilot program reports a 24% improvement, but participants volunteered and there was no comparison group. Which conclusion is best supported?

Your answer: The pilot is promising, but selection effects and the missing comparison prevent attributing the change to the program.

A before-and-after result without a comparison group cannot separate the program's effect from selection, time, or other changes.

✓ Correct

A chart shows a sharp rise after its vertical axis is truncated. What should you inspect before repeating its headline?

Your answer: The full axis scale, absolute values, time window, and denominator.

Axis range, denominator, and time window determine what a visual change means; a cropped axis can exaggerate its apparent size.

✓ Correct

A group-wide result improves, while one subgroup's result falls. What is the most responsible next analysis?

Your answer: Check subgroup sample sizes, uncertainty, baseline differences, and whether the intervention worked differently.

Aggregate results can mask uneven effects; subgroup estimates need uncertainty and baseline context before drawing conclusions.

✓ Correct

A project has a fixed budget and two proposals: one offers a large uncertain benefit, the other a smaller reliable benefit. What makes the decision auditable?

Your answer: Compare expected benefit, uncertainty, downside, and reversibility against an agreed objective.

A decision record that makes the objective, assumptions, uncertainty, and downside visible supports reasoned trade-offs.

✓ Correct

A screening tool is used where only 1 in 500 cases has the condition. It catches most true cases, but produces many false positives. What should be checked before interpreting a positive result?

Your answer: The positive predictive value at this prevalence and the consequences of follow-up testing.

When the condition is rare, even a sensitive tool can produce many false positives; predictive value depends on prevalence and error costs.

✓ Correct

A policy is evaluated using records from people who could access the service. Which limitation should be stated?

Your answer: The findings may not transfer to people excluded by access, eligibility, or missing records.

Large datasets can still omit systematically excluded populations; sample size does not establish representativeness.

✓ Correct

A training course has a high completion rate, but learners were allowed to stop reporting their outcomes at any time. Which follow-up best addresses the main uncertainty?

Your answer: Report who is missing and compare follow-up rates and outcomes across relevant groups.

Missing outcomes can be systematic; examining attrition and subgroup follow-up helps assess whether the reported result is biased.

✓ Correct

An API retry can arrive after the first request succeeded but before its response reached the caller. Which design best prevents a duplicate payment?

Your answer: Require an idempotency key and persist the original outcome for retries.

A stable idempotency key lets the service return the original result without repeating a non-idempotent side effect.

✓ Correct

A small experiment tests 20 outcomes and highlights the one with p < .05. What should strengthen the claim?

Your answer: A pre-specified primary outcome or an appropriate multiple-comparison correction, followed by replication.

Trying many outcomes increases false-positive risk; pre-specification, correction, and independent replication make the evidence more credible.
